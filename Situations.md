# Situations

> The issue finder's guide. Each situation lists the models that bear on it and why. Rules in CLAUDE.md.

## Projects and plans

### A project keeps running late
keywords: behind schedule, running late, delay, slipping, deadline, overrun, taking longer than expected, missed deadline, pushed back again, never on time, late project, falling behind
- [[Planning Fallacy]]: Forecasts for one's own plans start from the best case, so slippage is the norm rather than bad luck.
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]]: How long similar projects actually took is a better anchor than the plan for this one.
- [[Brooks's Law]]: Adding people late adds training and coordination load before it adds output, so the obvious rescue can deepen the delay.

### Estimating how long something will take
keywords: estimate, how long, quote, timeline, time estimate, budget estimate, guess, ballpark, forecast, how much will it cost, how much time, padding
- [[Planning Fallacy]]: An estimate built from the task list describes the best case, which is why so many run over.
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]]: How similar jobs actually turned out gives a starting point the plan's own details cannot.
- [[Anchoring]]: A first number put on the table, even a casual guess, tends to pull later estimates toward it.
- [[Margin of Safety]]: A gap between the estimate and the commitment absorbs errors without needing to know which ones will arrive.

### Adding people to catch up
keywords: more people, hire more, extra staff, throw bodies at it, add headcount, bring in help, contractors, staffing up, more hands, double the team, onboarding
- [[Brooks's Law]]: Newcomers cost time before they save any, and coordination grows faster than headcount on tightly linked work.
- [[Planning Fallacy]]: A project that looks late may never have been on schedule, so the shortfall may lie in the estimate, not the headcount.
- [[Second-Order Thinking]]: The first effect is more hands; the effects after that include training, handoffs and more meetings.

### About to launch and everyone's confident
keywords: launch, go live, rollout, everyone agrees, sure it will work, no objections, final plan, sign off, green light, what could go wrong, overconfident, groupthink
- [[Pre-Mortem]]: Imagining the plan has already failed makes it safe to name risks that confident teams otherwise keep quiet.
- [[Inversion]]: Asking what would guarantee failure surfaces weak spots that asking how to succeed walks right past.
- [[Confirmation Bias]]: A team that already believes in the plan seeks and remembers evidence that it will work.
- [[WYSIATI]]: Confidence tracks how well the story fits together, not how much of the picture is missing.

### The plan no longer matches what's happening
keywords: plan is outdated, things changed, off track, reality is different, sticking to the plan, out of date, assumptions wrong, spreadsheet says, roadmap, pivot, plan vs reality
- [[The Map Is Not the Territory]]: A plan is a map drawn at one moment, and trouble starts when it is still followed after the ground has moved.
- [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]]: Effort already poured into the plan is gone either way, so it says nothing about whether the plan still fits.
- [[Confirmation Bias]]: Evidence that the plan is still working gets noticed while evidence that it isn't quietly gets forgotten.

### Cutting the buffer from a budget or schedule
keywords: slack, buffer, contingency, padding, cushion, trim the budget, tighten the schedule, safety margin, too much time, lean, cut contingency, extra time, no room for error
- [[Margin of Safety]]: Slack absorbs errors and bad luck that nobody can name in advance, which is exactly why it looks wasteful.
- [[Parkinson's Law]]: Generous time and money tend to get used up whether needed or not, so some padding is just room to expand.
- [[Planning Fallacy]]: Estimates already lean optimistic, so the buffer may be covering a real shortfall rather than fat.

## Money and investing

### A friend got rich on a hot stock
keywords: hot stock, crypto, got rich, made a fortune, fomo, missing out, everyone's buying, stock tip, meme stock, to the moon, easy money, getting in late, jealous
- [[Survivorship Bias]]: The friends who lost money on similar bets rarely tell the story, so the visible winners look like the norm.
- [[Availability Heuristic]]: One vivid, recent win comes to mind easily and makes big gains feel more common than they are.
- [[Resulting]]: A great outcome does not show the bet was sound; luck and hidden information may have done the work.
- [[Circle of Competence]]: Hearing about someone else's win is not the same as having tested judgment in this kind of bet.

### Holding a losing investment, hoping it recovers
keywords: losing money, should I sell, in the red, wait till it comes back, break even, can't sell at a loss, bag holding, it'll recover, paper loss, stock dropped, what I paid, get my money back, stock is down, shares are down, portfolio is down
- [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]]: Money already lost is gone either way, so only where the investment goes from here bears on the choice.
- [[Loss Aversion]]: Selling turns a paper loss into a sure one, which tends to hurt more than an equal gain pleases, though by how much is debated.
- [[Anchoring]]: The purchase price anchors judgments of what the investment is worth, though the market neither knows nor cares what was paid.

### An adviser is pushing a financial product
keywords: financial adviser, advisor, broker, salesperson, commission, insurance, annuity, fees, can I trust my adviser, they say it's best, upsell, bank rep, fund pitch
- [[Incentive-Caused Bias]]: Advice from someone paid by the recommendation bends toward it, usually sincerely and without the adviser noticing.
- [[Circle of Competence]]: The adviser knows the product far better than the buyer, so the buyer is judging from outside the circle where judgment is reliable.
- [[Survivorship Bias]]: A fund's glowing track record can come from a lineup where the losing funds were quietly closed.

### A fund had a spectacular year
keywords: top fund, best performer, record year, beat the market, star manager, chasing returns, last year's winner, hot streak, top ranked, five stars, past performance
- [[Regression to the Mean]]: An extreme year owes part of its height to luck, and the luck tends not to repeat.
- [[Survivorship Bias]]: Rankings show the funds still standing, not the ones that were closed after bad years.
- [[Resulting]]: One great outcome is a small sample, so it grades the luck as much as the decisions.

### Wondering if a big purchase is worth it
keywords: big purchase, can I afford it, worth it, splurge, new car, renovation, expensive, on sale, discount, list price, upgrade, treat myself, buyer's remorse, impulse buy
- [[Opportunity Cost]]: The real price is the best other use of that money, which no price tag shows.
- [[Anchoring]]: A high list price or "was" price can make the sale price feel cheap whether or not it is.
- [[Margin of Safety]]: It shifts the question from whether the purchase is affordable to whether finances still hold up if income or costs turn out worse.

### How much emergency savings is enough
keywords: emergency fund, savings, rainy day, cushion, safety net, cash on hand, job loss, unexpected bills, too much cash, idle money, just in case, how many months
- [[Margin of Safety]]: A cash gap absorbs shocks nobody can predict, which is the whole point of holding it.
- [[Opportunity Cost]]: Money parked as a cushion is money not doing something else, so safety has a quiet price.

### Negotiating a price or a salary
keywords: negotiate, haggle, salary, raise, offer, counteroffer, asking price, lowball, first offer, buying a car, rent, bargaining, what to ask for
- [[Anchoring]]: The first number on the table tends to pull the final one toward it, even when it was plainly arbitrary.
- [[Loss Aversion]]: The same term can feel costlier framed as something given up than as something gained, though how much costlier is debated.

### Everyone says prices can only go up
keywords: housing market, prices only go up, bubble, boom, can't lose, real estate, property, never been a better time, everyone's buying, it's different this time, mania, crash
- [[Reflexivity]]: Belief that prices will rise drives buying that raises prices, a loop that can overshoot and reverse.
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]]: How past booms in similar markets ended is evidence the current story tends to leave out.
- [[Margin of Safety]]: A purchase that works only if prices keep rising leaves no room for the forecast being wrong.

## Deciding and choosing

### Deciding whether to quit something
keywords: quit, give up, walk away, stick with it, keep going, already invested, too far in, throwing good money after bad, can't let go, wasted years, stay or go, cut my losses, feel trapped
- [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]]: Past time and money are gone either way, so only future costs and benefits bear on the choice.
- [[Opportunity Cost]]: Staying has a price too: whatever else that time, money and attention could be doing.
- [[Loss Aversion]]: Quitting feels like locking in a loss, which can weigh more than an equal gain from moving on.

### Choosing between two job offers
keywords: job offer, two offers, career move, switch jobs, new job, stay or leave, which job, career change, grass is greener, counteroffer, accept the offer, better pay
- [[Opportunity Cost]]: Taking either offer means giving up the other, so each one's real cost is what the other would have provided.
- [[WYSIATI]]: Each option is judged from the story available, and an interview shows far less than a year in the job will.
- [[Loss Aversion]]: If one option is staying put, what the current job provides tends to feel bigger as a loss than the same amount gained elsewhere.

### Stuck on a problem and going in circles
keywords: stuck, going in circles, can't solve it, can't figure it out, blocked, dead end, out of ideas, tried everything, brainstorm, fresh angle, overthinking, spinning wheels
- [[Inversion]]: Working backward from what would guarantee failure can open a route when the direct one is stuck.
- [[First Principles Thinking]]: Stripping the problem to facts that cannot be reduced further exposes which constraints are real and which are habit.

### Old and proven, or new and shiny
keywords: new tool, latest, trendy, proven, tried and true, old school, upgrade, switch to, fad, cutting edge, hype, legacy, new method, classic
- [[Lindy Effect]]: For ideas and tools that do not wear out, a long survival record is evidence, not proof, of more survival ahead.
- [[First Principles Thinking]]: When the situation is genuinely new, rebuilding from basic facts can beat copying what has always been done.

### Saying yes to one more commitment
keywords: overcommitted, too busy, can't say no, one more thing, volunteer, favour, side project, spread too thin, stretched thin, too much on my plate, overwhelmed, burnout, burned out, exhausted, people pleaser
- [[Opportunity Cost]]: Every yes carries a silent no to whatever that time and attention would otherwise have gone to.
- [[Planning Fallacy]]: The new commitment gets sized from its best case, so it usually takes more time than it seemed.

## People, motives and conflict

### Someone did something that hurt or annoyed me
keywords: offended, upset, rude, betrayed, ignored, slighted, did it on purpose, angry at someone, disrespected, snubbed, left out, why would they, hurt feelings
- [[Hanlon's Razor]]: Error, overload and missing information cause far more harm than ill will does, which makes them the likelier first explanation.
- [[Incentive-Caused Bias]]: The behaviour may follow what the person's role rewards, with no malice involved.
- [[WYSIATI]]: The story of why they did it is built from what is visible, and their side is mostly invisible.

### Someone else takes credit for my work
keywords: takes credit, took credit, stole my idea, credit for my work, boss takes credit, not recognized, overlooked, presented my work as theirs, nobody noticed, unfair recognition
- [[WYSIATI]]: Whoever presents the work is what decision makers see, and they rarely ask what is missing from that picture.
- [[Incentive-Caused Bias]]: Where reward follows visibility, people sincerely come to see the visible role as the important one.
- [[Hanlon's Razor]]: Muddled memory or a careless summary can misplace credit without anyone setting out to steal it.

### The same person keeps doing this to me
keywords: every time, pattern, keeps happening, always does this, repeat offender, can't trust them, fool me twice, on purpose, sabotage, taking advantage, toxic, fed up
- [[Hanlon's Razor]]: The razor stops fitting when a pattern repeats and keeps benefiting the same party.
- [[Confirmation Bias]]: Once a motive is suspected, every new incident gets read as proof and the innocent ones get forgotten.
- [[Incentive-Caused Bias]]: A repeating pattern may track what the person is rewarded for rather than a personal grudge.

### A contractor says I need expensive repairs
keywords: contractor, mechanic, dentist, plumber, repairs, upsell, second opinion, do I really need it, quote, ripped off, overcharged, service call, too expensive, scam
- [[Incentive-Caused Bias]]: Someone paid by the repair can sincerely see more need for it than a neutral party would.
- [[Circle of Competence]]: The expert knows the domain far better than the customer, which is exactly what makes the advice hard to judge.
- [[Anchoring]]: The first quote tends to anchor what a fair price seems to be, so later quotes get judged against it.

### We argue and neither side budges
keywords: argument, disagreement, debate, won't listen, stubborn, fight, politics, family argument, can't agree, talking past each other, closed minded, agree to disagree, can't convince them
- [[Confirmation Bias]]: Both sides read the same evidence as support for what they already believed.
- [[WYSIATI]]: Each side builds a coherent story from the information it has and treats it as complete.
- [[The Map Is Not the Territory]]: When labels are doing the arguing, the fight is often over the words rather than the thing.

### Sizing someone up from a first impression
keywords: first impression, gut feeling, stereotype, seems like the type, judging people, vibe, profile, judge a book by its cover, prejudice, snap judgment, read people, sizing up
- [[Representativeness Heuristic]]: Fitting a type feels like likelihood, while how common the type actually is gets ignored.
- [[WYSIATI]]: A few minutes of information produces a coherent story, and the story feels complete.
- [[Confirmation Bias]]: Once the impression forms, later evidence gets read in its favour.

## Work, teams and management

### A metric looks great but something feels off
keywords: kpi, metric, numbers look good, dashboard, targets met, gaming the numbers, vanity metric, all green, too good to be true, stats, scorecard, report looks great
- [[Goodhart's Law]]: Once people are rewarded by a number, effort flows into the number instead of what it was meant to track.
- [[The Map Is Not the Territory]]: A metric is a map of the work, leaving most of it out by design.
- [[Incentive-Caused Bias]]: The people reporting the number may sincerely see it more favourably because their pay or standing depends on it.

### Setting targets or bonuses for a team
keywords: targets, quotas, bonus, incentive plan, performance pay, reward, commission, objectives, okrs, goals for the team, pay for performance, my boss wants numbers
- [[Goodhart's Law]]: A number tied to pay tends to track its goal less well once people chase it, though by how much varies.
- [[Second-Order Thinking]]: The first effect is more effort; the next is people adapting to the rule and gaming it.
- [[Incentive-Caused Bias]]: People's judgment bends toward whatever the bonus rewards, usually without them noticing.

### Meetings eat the whole day
keywords: meetings, calendar full, too many meetings, busywork, status meeting, back to back, wasted time, an hour for a ten minute thing, always busy, time sink, endless meetings
- [[Parkinson's Law]]: A meeting booked for an hour tends to take the hour, whatever the agenda needs.
- [[Opportunity Cost]]: Every hour in a meeting is an hour unavailable for the work the meeting was about.

### Criticism seems to work better than praise
keywords: praise, criticism, feedback, performance review, yelling works, tough love, coaching, after a great month, after a bad month, reward vs punish, manager, slump
- [[Regression to the Mean]]: Performance after an unusually good or bad stretch drifts toward average anyway, making criticism look effective and praise look harmful.
- [[Resulting]]: Judging feedback by the next result credits the feedback for what luck did.

### Hiring someone after a great interview
keywords: hiring, interview, candidate, resume, great fit, impressive, references, gut feel, charismatic, new hire, recruit, bad hire, looked perfect on paper
- [[WYSIATI]]: A polished résumé and a smooth interview form a coherent story, and confidence ignores how much is missing.
- [[Representativeness Heuristic]]: A candidate who looks like a star gets judged by resemblance rather than how often such candidates succeed.
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]]: How past hires with similar profiles actually performed is evidence the interview cannot provide.

### Moving into an area we don't know well
keywords: new market, expand, diversify, outside our expertise, new field, branch out, unfamiliar, new industry, side hustle, out of my depth, beginner, never done this
- [[Circle of Competence]]: An advantage inside one domain can vanish just outside it, and the edge is hard to see from within.
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]]: How others' moves into similar territory turned out is a check on the inside story.
- [[First Principles Thinking]]: Reasoning from basic facts can help when old analogies mislead, though it discards conventions that hold know-how a newcomer lacks.

## Judging evidence, news and claims

### A news story makes something seem common
keywords: news, headline, scary, everyone's talking about it, trending, viral, crime, plane crash, shark attack, seems like it's everywhere, media, doomscrolling, how likely is it
- [[Availability Heuristic]]: Vivid, recent, heavily reported events come to mind easily, which makes them feel more frequent than they are.
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]]: How often the thing happens across the whole population is the number the headline usually leaves out.
- [[WYSIATI]]: A gripping story feels complete and pushes aside the dull statistics that would put it in proportion.

### An article proves exactly what I believed
keywords: study shows, I knew it, proof, research says, finally someone said it, agrees with me, echo chamber, my side, cherry picked, biased source, told you so
- [[Confirmation Bias]]: Evidence that agrees gets accepted quickly, while evidence that disagrees gets scrutinized or forgotten.
- [[WYSIATI]]: One coherent article feels like the whole picture, with no sense of what was left out.

### A worrying test result for something rare
keywords: test result, positive test, screening, diagnosis, false positive, medical test, rare disease, scary result, lab results, accuracy, do I have it, worried sick
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]]: When a condition is rare, how common it is weighs heavily on what a positive result actually means.
- [[Representativeness Heuristic]]: A result that looks like the disease feels like proof, while the prior odds get neglected.

### A theory keeps needing extra excuses
keywords: conspiracy, cover up, too complicated, far fetched, makes excuses, explains everything, rabbit hole, connecting the dots, they're hiding it, plot, secret plan, doesn't add up
- [[Occam's Razor]]: A story that keeps needing patches assumes more than the plain explanation that fits the same facts.
- [[Confirmation Bias]]: Each new fact gets bent to fit the theory, and facts that don't fit quietly disappear.
- [[Hanlon's Razor]]: Error and confusion are far more common than coordinated intent, though the razor is a starting assumption, not a verdict.

### A friend swears it worked for them
keywords: anecdote, it worked for me, testimonial, word of mouth, my friend tried it, success story, miracle cure, diet, supplement, life hack, reviews, swears by it
- [[Representativeness Heuristic]]: One vivid case gets treated like a representative sample, an overreaction to very small numbers.
- [[Survivorship Bias]]: The people it failed for rarely mention it, so the stories that reach anyone are the successes.
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]]: How often it works across everyone who tried it is the number one story cannot supply.
- [[Regression to the Mean]]: A remedy tried when things are at their worst can get credit for a recovery that was coming anyway.

### A celebrity gives advice outside their field
keywords: celebrity, famous, influencer, ceo says, billionaire advice, expert opinion, podcast, endorsement, sponsored, guru, they're successful so, thought leader
- [[Circle of Competence]]: Expertise holds inside its boundary and vanishes outside it, however famous the expert.
- [[Survivorship Bias]]: Success makes the advice sound proven, but the many who did the same and failed aren't on the podcast.
- [[Incentive-Caused Bias]]: Paid endorsements and personal brands reward certain beliefs, and judgment bends sincerely toward what pays.

### Trying to figure out why something broke
keywords: root cause, what went wrong, troubleshooting, debugging, it stopped working, bug, outage, who broke it, why did it fail, keeps breaking, keeps crashing, broken, glitch, investigating
- [[Occam's Razor]]: When several causes fit the symptoms equally well, the one assuming least is the natural first to test.
- [[Confirmation Bias]]: Investigations tend to lock onto the first suspect and read later clues in its favour.

## Learning from success and failure

### A good decision turned out badly
keywords: bad outcome, it backfired, regret, made the wrong call, unlucky, beating myself up, second guessing, it didn't work out, mistake, bad luck, did the right thing
- [[Resulting]]: A bad outcome does not prove a bad decision; luck and hidden information also shape results.
- [[Hindsight Bias]]: Knowing the ending makes it look more predictable than it was at the moment of choosing.

### Everyone says it was obvious all along
keywords: should have seen it coming, obvious in hindsight, blame, post mortem, who's responsible, red flags, I told you so, finger pointing, lessons learned, retrospective, should have known, I knew it, knew it would happen, saw it coming
- [[Hindsight Bias]]: Once the outcome is known, people overestimate what they would have predicted and misremember what they did predict.
- [[Resulting]]: Judging the decision by its outcome blames choices for what luck or missing information did.
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]]: How often such warning signs came before such outcomes, judged beforehand, is the test of whether it really was obvious.
- [[Hanlon's Razor]]: When blame turns personal, error, overload and missing information are more common causes than anyone wanting it to fail.

### I won, but was it skill or luck
keywords: lucky, skill, winning streak, am I good, beginner's luck, it worked, got rewarded, nailed it, one win, overconfident, track record, talent
- [[Resulting]]: A good outcome does not certify a good decision, especially from a sample of one.
- [[Regression to the Mean]]: The more chance in a result, the more the next one tends to land closer to average.
- [[Circle of Competence]]: One win is not the same as judgment that has been tested on this kind of problem.

### Things improved right after we stepped in
keywords: fixed it, turned around, recovered, intervention, new policy, after we changed, bounced back, credit, coincidence, before and after, rebound, it worked
- [[Regression to the Mean]]: Things picked for action because they were at their worst tend to improve anyway as luck stops repeating.
- [[Confirmation Bias]]: Those who made the change look for signs it worked and give the improvement to their fix.

### Copying what successful people did
keywords: habits of successful people, morning routine, secret to success, how they made it, role model, best practices, copy, follow their path, dropout billionaire, success stories, self help, how to be successful
- [[Survivorship Bias]]: The successful are studied because they succeeded, while those with the same habits who failed are out of sight.
- [[Resulting]]: Crediting the habits for the outcome assumes luck and circumstance played no part.

### I got away with a risky shortcut
keywords: got away with it, near miss, cut corners, shortcut, it was fine, nothing happened, lucky break, close call, risky, no harm done, did it anyway
- [[Resulting]]: A fine outcome does not make the risk small; the dice may simply have landed kindly.
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]]: How often the shortcut goes wrong across many tries says more than how it went this time.
- [[Margin of Safety]]: A near miss shows how little gap there was between the plan and failure.

## Risk and the future

### Something feels like a sure thing
keywords: sure thing, can't lose, guaranteed, no brainer, too good to be true, obvious bet, all in, certain, risk free, slam dunk, feels like a sure thing, bet the farm
- [[WYSIATI]]: Certainty tracks how well the story hangs together, not how much information is missing.
- [[Margin of Safety]]: It asks whether things survive if the estimate is badly wrong, a question that feeling sure cannot answer.
- [[Representativeness Heuristic]]: A case that looks just like past winners feels likely, though resemblance is not probability.
- [[Circle of Competence]]: Feeling sure and having tested judgment in this domain are different things.

### Making a big call I can't undo
keywords: irreversible, no going back, big decision, life changing, permanent, all or nothing, high stakes, can't take it back, sign the contract, burn the boats, point of no return, final answer, big decision, worried about a decision, anxious about a choice
- [[Margin of Safety]]: When failure cannot be reversed, the gap between what is expected and what is risked is what absorbs being wrong.
- [[Pre-Mortem]]: Picturing the choice as having already failed surfaces reasons while there is still time to act on them.
- [[Second-Order Thinking]]: The effects that follow from the first effect are where hard-to-undo choices tend to surprise.
- [[Circle of Competence]]: High stakes and hard reversal are exactly when the edge of one's knowledge matters most.

### Wondering if a trend will last
keywords: fad, trend, will it last, passing phase, here to stay, new technology, craze, flash in the pan, hype, future, staying power, the next big thing
- [[Lindy Effect]]: For things that do not wear out, a long track record hints at more future survival than a recent arrival has.
- [[Reflexivity]]: Belief in a trend can feed the trend itself until it overshoots and reverses.
- [[Availability Heuristic]]: A trend everyone is talking about feels bigger and more lasting than it may be.

### Panic buying because everyone else is
keywords: panic buying, shortage, bank run, stockpile, everyone's selling, stampede, herd, toilet paper, sell off, contagion, fear spreading, empty shelves
- [[Reflexivity]]: The belief that something will run out makes people buy, which makes it run out.
- [[Second-Order Thinking]]: Each person's sensible reaction becomes everyone's problem once others respond in turn.
- [[Availability Heuristic]]: Images of empty shelves can make scarcity feel more widespread than it is.

### Forecasting next year from this year's numbers
keywords: forecast, projection, next year, budget, last year's numbers, growth rate, extrapolate, trend line, plan for next year, prediction, outlook, record year
- [[Regression to the Mean]]: An unusually good or bad year partly reflects luck that is unlikely to repeat.
- [[Anchoring]]: Last year's figure can hold the forecast closer to it than new evidence justifies, so many forecasts barely move.
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]]: How similar businesses or years actually performed offers a check on the extrapolation.
- [[The Map Is Not the Territory]]: A forecast built on past conditions stops fitting once the conditions shift.

## Changing or removing rules and systems

### Wanting to scrap a pointless rule
keywords: pointless rule, red tape, bureaucracy, stupid policy, why do we do this, get rid of, scrap it, outdated rule, nobody knows why, makes no sense, cut the rule, always done it this way
- [[Chesterton's Fence]]: Not seeing a rule's purpose says something about the reformer's knowledge, not about the rule.
- [[Lindy Effect]]: A rule that has survived a long time has some evidence of usefulness behind it, though not proof.
- [[First Principles Thinking]]: Separating real constraints from ones that are only habit shows whether the rule rests on anything solid.
- [[Second-Order Thinking]]: Removing the rule changes how people behave, and those reactions are part of the result.

### Rewriting something from scratch
keywords: start over, rewrite, rebuild, clean slate, from scratch, tear it down, overhaul, redo, legacy code, reinvent, burn it down, fresh start, new system
- [[Chesterton's Fence]]: Odd parts of an old system often exist for reasons that surface only after they are gone.
- [[First Principles Thinking]]: Rebuilding from basic facts can drop costs that rest on habit rather than necessity.
- [[Planning Fallacy]]: The rewrite gets estimated from its best case, so it tends to take longer than planned.
- [[Lindy Effect]]: Long-lived systems have survived tests the replacement has not faced yet.

### Banning or taxing something to fix a problem
keywords: ban, tax, crackdown, new law, regulation, prohibition, unintended consequences, backfire, policy change, outlaw, restrict, workaround, loophole
- [[Second-Order Thinking]]: People adapt to a ban or a tax, and their adaptations can outweigh the first effect.
- [[Inversion]]: Asking how the measure could make things worse surfaces failure modes that the hopeful version misses.

### Cutting a step to save time or money
keywords: cost cutting, streamline, efficiency, remove a step, simplify, skip a check, redundant, waste, trim, save money, unnecessary, double checking
- [[Chesterton's Fence]]: A step that looks redundant may guard against a problem nobody has seen lately precisely because the step works.
- [[Margin of Safety]]: Redundancy is often the slack that absorbs errors, so it looks like waste until it is needed.
- [[Second-Order Thinking]]: The saving is the first effect; what happens when the removed step's job goes undone comes later.

### The official process isn't how things really work
keywords: org chart, official process, on paper, policy manual, how it really works, unwritten rules, workaround, documentation is wrong, procedures, informal, shadow process
- [[The Map Is Not the Territory]]: The documented process is a map, and the work may have moved since anyone checked it against the ground.
- [[Chesterton's Fence]]: Unofficial workarounds often exist for reasons worth knowing before they get stamped out.
