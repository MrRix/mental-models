## What it is
"Never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity." When harm could stem from carelessness, ignorance or error, the razor puts that explanation first, because it is far more common than deliberate ill will.

## Origin
The line appeared, labelled "Hanlon's Razor", in Arthur Bloch's *Murphy's Law Book Two* (1980). Quote Investigator credits Robert J. Hanlon, a Scranton, Pennsylvania computer programmer who submitted it to Bloch; the attribution rests partly on accounts from his widow and son. The idea is older: Goethe's 1774 *Werther* blames misunderstanding and neglect over malice, and Heinlein's 1941 story "Logic of Empire" makes a similar point, which led some to suspect "Hanlon" was a garbled "Heinlein". Credit to Napoleon is unsupported.

## Why it works
People overread intent: a bad outcome feels designed, especially when it hurts. But a scheme needs motive, planning and secrecy, while a mistake needs only a busy person. Errors, overload and bad information vastly outnumber schemes, and starting from that base rate keeps blame proportionate.

## Best used when
- Someone causes harm with no clear motive.
- Evidence of intent is thin and a wrong accusation is costly.
- A delay or omission could easily be an oversight.

## Limitations
- It is a prior, not a verdict. Real malice exists and sometimes hides behind apparent incompetence.
- Malice versus stupidity is a false binary; [[Incentive-Caused Bias]] produces harm with neither.
- "Stupidity" is a crude label for what is often bad design or missing information.

## Avoid when
- Security and fraud, where attackers rely on being mistaken for error.
- A pattern repeats and consistently benefits the same party.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Incentive-Caused Bias]]: look at what the system rewards, not intent or intelligence.
- [[Fundamental Attribution Error]]: blaming character over circumstances, which Hanlon only half corrects.
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]]: the statistical case behind the razor.

## Who used it
- The Jargon File, the hacker lexicon, which added it in 1990 and carried it through computing culture.
- Robert Byrne's 1994 quotation compilation, which printed a version credited to one Nick Diamos, a sign of how loosely the line circulated.

## Trigger question
Is there a plausible explanation for this that involves no intent at all?

## Often confused with
- [[Occam's Razor]]: general parsimony; Hanlon applies a similar tiebreak to motives.
- Assuming good faith: a courtesy norm, not a claim about likelihood.
