## What it is
A margin of safety is a deliberate gap between what something is estimated to withstand (or be worth) and what it is actually asked to bear (or cost). The gap absorbs errors and bad luck without anyone needing to know in advance which will arrive.

## Origin
Engineers had long built structures to carry several times the expected load. The investing term comes from Benjamin Graham and David Dodd of Columbia, in *Security Analysis* (1934). They applied it to bonds, whose issuers' earnings should cover interest with room to spare, and to stocks bought well below conservative value. Graham made it the centrepiece of *The Intelligent Investor* (1949), whose closing chapter in later editions calls it the central concept of investment. Warren Buffett's 1984 essay "The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville" restated it with a bridge built for 30,000 pounds but crossed only by 10,000-pound trucks.

## Why it works
Estimates are wrong in unpredictable ways, and the errors are rarely symmetric in cost. One buffer covers analytical mistakes, bad luck and unknown unknowns at once, with no need to diagnose which is coming. Its price is usually modest; the failure it prevents is often ruinous and irreversible.

## Best used when
- Estimates are uncertain and the forecaster's bias is unknown.
- Failure is irreversible: bankruptcy, collapse, a deadline that cannot slip.

## Limitations
- The buffer is only as good as the estimate beneath it. Buying 40% below a wrong value is no safety, which is how value traps work.
- Safety costs something: idle cash, heavier structures, padded schedules.

## Avoid when
- Experiments are cheap and reversible, so speed beats protection.
- Competition punishes slack and failure is survivable.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Opportunity Cost]]: the main tension, since every unit of buffer is a unit not deployed.
- Efficiency and just-in-time thinking: strip margins out on purpose.
- [[Inversion]]: shows what the margin must guard against.

## Who used it
- Benjamin Graham, at Graham-Newman.
- Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger at Berkshire Hathaway.
- Seth Klarman, who titled his 1991 book *Margin of Safety*.

## Trigger question
If this estimate is badly wrong, does the plan still survive?

## Often confused with
- Margin of safety in accounting: how far sales can fall before break-even.
- [[Redundancy]]: a spare copy, rather than slack within one thing.
