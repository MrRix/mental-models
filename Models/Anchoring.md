## What it is
When people estimate an uncertain number, a value they have just considered pulls the estimate toward it, even when that value is plainly irrelevant. The anchor does not need to be credible; it only needs to be thought about.

## Origin
Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman described "anchoring and adjustment" in "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases" (*Science*, 1974). A wheel of fortune rigged to stop at 10 or 65 was spun before people estimated the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. Median estimates were 25 and 45 percent respectively, and paying for accuracy did not remove the effect.

## Why it works
Two mechanisms are proposed. In insufficient adjustment, people start at the anchor and move away until they reach a plausible value, then stop, so they land on the near edge of the plausible range. In selective accessibility, testing whether the answer could be near the anchor makes anchor-consistent facts easier to recall, and those facts then shape the estimate.

## Best used when
- Explaining why first offers, list prices and opening estimates carry so much weight.
- Diagnosing forecasts that barely moved from last year's figure.

## Limitations
Standard anchoring, where people explicitly compare against the anchor, is among the most replicated findings in judgment research and held up in the Many Labs project. Weaker forms are shaky. Incidental anchors, numbers merely present in the environment, failed in Many Labs 2 and in a replication by Shanks and colleagues. Subliminal anchoring failed two preregistered replications by Röseler and colleagues.

## Avoid when
- The prior number is relevant evidence rather than an arbitrary starting point.
- The only suspect is a stray background number nobody compared against.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]] deliberately anchors on a reference class, so the problem is a bad anchor, not anchoring as such.
- Anchoring on the plan itself feeds the [[Planning Fallacy]].

## Who used it
Englich, Mussweiler and Strack (2006) found experienced legal professionals' sentences shifted toward a sentencing demand, even when the judges set it by throwing dice. Galinsky and Mussweiler (2001) found first offers strongly predicted final prices in negotiations.

## Trigger question
Would the answer be the same if the first number had been different?

## Often confused with
- [[Confirmation Bias]]: seeking support for a belief, not distorting a number.
- [[Framing Effect]]: changes how options are described, not the reference number.
