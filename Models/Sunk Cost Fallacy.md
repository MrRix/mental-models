## What it is
Continuing something because of what has already gone into it, rather than what it will return from here. Money, time and effort already spent are gone whichever way the decision goes, so in standard economics they should not affect it.

## Origin
Richard Dawkins and T. Carlisle named the "Concorde fallacy" in *Nature* in 1976, about animal parental investment. Richard Thaler described the human version in 1980: a family drives through a snowstorm to a basketball game because they paid for tickets. Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer tested it in 1985: theatre-goers randomly given discounted season tickets attended less in the first half-season than full-price buyers.

## Why it works
Quitting turns a paper loss into a realized one and concedes that the spending was wasted. Arkes and Blumer traced the effect to not wanting to appear wasteful. [[Loss Aversion]] adds that a sure loss hurts more than a gamble that might win it back, so pressing on feels cheaper than stopping.

## Best used when
- Explaining why failing projects, films or wars continue after their prospects collapse.
- A decision is justified by what was "put in" rather than what comes out.

## Limitations
- The evidence is weaker than its fame. Vignettes replicate, but incentivized experiments are mixed; Negrini, Riedl and Wibral (2020) found a *reverse* sunk cost bias with real money.
- Honouring sunk costs can be rational for reasons an observer cannot see, such as avoiding blame or acting on private information.

## Avoid when
- Past spending lowers future costs, as with a nearly finished building.
- Commitment itself signals resolve, and a reputation for persistence is worth more than the project.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Opportunity Cost]]: the only live question is what the same resources could do now.
- Grit: many successes look like sunk-cost errors until they pay off; judging which was which after the fact invites [[Resulting]].

## Who used it
- Concorde: Britain and France kept funding the jet after its economic case collapsed.
- Andy Grove and Gordon Moore took Intel out of memory chips in 1985 by asking what a newly hired CEO would do.

## Trigger question
If none of this had been spent yet, would anyone start it today?

## Often confused with
- [[Loss Aversion]], a broader mechanism that partly explains it.
- Escalation of commitment (Barry Staw), which adds self-justification.
