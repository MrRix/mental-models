## What it is
People predict that their own projects will take less time and money than they actually do, even after similar projects overran. The forecast is built from the plan, and a plan describes the best case.

## Origin
Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky named it in "Intuitive Prediction: Biases and Corrective Procedures" (1979). The founding case was Kahneman's team writing an Israeli school curriculum: members guessed about two years, similar projects implied seven to ten, and it took eight. Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross added experiments in 1994: students predicted their theses would take about 34 days; the average was 55.5.

## Why it works
Forecasters take the *inside view*: they simulate the steps of this particular plan. A plan only contains what someone thought of, so it omits the delays nobody can name in advance. Each is unlikely, but with so many candidates some are nearly certain. Memory files past overruns as one-off bad luck, not a pattern.

## Best used when
- Explaining why projects, renovations and public works routinely run late and over budget.
- A schedule is built bottom-up from task lists, with no reference to how similar projects ended.

## Limitations
- It concerns one's own tasks; Buehler's observers tended to overestimate other people's times.
- Anonymous predictions showed no optimistic bias in one experiment, hinting at impression management.
- Bent Flyvbjerg argued many megaproject overruns are *strategic misrepresentation*, estimates lowballed to win approval.

## Avoid when
- The estimator profits from a low number, as a bidder or a project champion does; [[Incentive-Caused Bias]] fits better than optimism.
- The task is so new that no reference class defines "normal."

## Counter-models and tensions
[[Base Rates and the Outside View]] is the classic correction: set the plan's details aside and ask how similar projects ended. Flyvbjerg developed it into reference class forecasting. [[Parkinson's Law]] pulls the other way: padded deadlines get filled.

## Who used it
- Flyvbjerg applied reference class forecasting to infrastructure; UK Treasury appraisal guidance adds "optimism bias" uplifts.
- Sydney Opera House: due in 1963 at about $7 million, opened in 1973 at $102 million.

## Trigger question
How long did the last few projects like this actually take?

## Often confused with
- Hofstadter's Law, Douglas Hofstadter's 1979 joke that work overruns even when the estimate allows for overruns.
- [[Parkinson's Law]], about how work behaves once a deadline is set, not how the deadline was forecast.
