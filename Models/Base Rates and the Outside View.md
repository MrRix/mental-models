## What it is
A base rate is how often something happens across a relevant population. The outside view predicts a case by first asking how similar cases turned out, then adjusting for specifics. The inside view builds a forecast from the case's own details and plan, and people reliably favour it.

## Origin
Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky documented base-rate neglect in "On the Psychology of Prediction" (*Psychological Review*, 1973). In their "Tom W." study, people matched a nerdy profile to computer science while ignoring far larger enrolments elsewhere. Their report "Intuitive Prediction: Biases and Corrective Procedures" (1977, published 1979) proposed a fix: choose a reference class, assess its outcomes, then adjust. Kahneman and Dan Lovallo named the "inside view" and "outside view" in "Timid Choices and Bold Forecasts" (*Management Science*, 1993).

## Why it works
Detail feels informative but rarely captures the many ways things go wrong. A reference class already contains the delays and failures no single plan anticipates. The base rate supplies a grounded prior; specifics then move it, usually less than intuition wants.

## Best used when
- Forecasting the cost, timeline or odds of success of projects and ventures.
- Interpreting a positive test for a rare condition.

## Limitations
Everything hinges on the reference class, and the original procedure offered little guidance on choosing one. Novel cases may have no meaningful class, and base rates drift as conditions change.

## Avoid when
- Specific evidence is strong and highly diagnostic.
- The only base rate comes from a population that differs in ways that matter.

## Counter-models and tensions
- It corrects the [[Planning Fallacy]] and the [[Representativeness Heuristic]].
- It clashes with [[First Principles Thinking]], which reasons from a case's own mechanics.
- A poorly chosen reference class becomes a bad anchor, the failure mode of [[Anchoring]].

## Who used it
Kahneman recounts a curriculum team he was on in Israel that forecast about two years to finish. Seymour Fox, a curriculum expert on the team, knew of no similar team that finished in under seven years; it took eight. After UK Treasury guidance on optimism bias, Bent Flyvbjerg built reference class uplifts for the Department for Transport; in 2004 reviewers applied them to Edinburgh's trams.

## Trigger question
What usually happens to cases like this one?

## Often confused with
- [[Regression to the Mean]]: a statistical pattern; the outside view is a forecasting method.
- Pessimism: the outside view aims at calibration, not gloom.
