## What it is
For things that do not age biologically, such as books, ideas, technologies and institutions, the longer something has survived, the longer it can be expected to keep surviving. A book in print for a century likely outlasts one published last year.

## Origin
The name comes from Lindy's, a New York deli where comedians gathered. In "Lindy's Law" (*The New Republic*, 1964), critic Albert Goldman reported their folk theory: a comic has a fixed stock of material, so more TV exposure means a faster burnout. That is nearly the opposite of the modern idea. Benoit Mandelbrot reused the name in *The Fractal Geometry of Nature* (1982), arguing that for some bodies of work, expected future output is proportional to past output. Nassim Nicholas Taleb called it the "Lindy effect" in *Antifragile* (2012) and extended it to anything nonperishable.

## Why it works
For nonperishables, survival is evidence: each year lasted is another round of tests passed against changing tastes, competitors and shocks. Statistically, the effect holds when lifetimes follow a heavy-tailed (Pareto-like) distribution, where expected remaining life grows with current age.

## Best used when
- Comparing old and new ideas, tools or texts with little other information.
- Separating fads from durable practices.

## Limitations
It is a prior, not a law, and the heavy-tail assumption may not hold. It measures persistence, not quality, and old things can fail abruptly when their environment changes.

## Avoid when
- The thing wears out (bodies, machines, batteries): a 90-year-old has fewer expected years than a 10-year-old.
- There is direct evidence about the thing's health.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Survivorship Bias]]: only the old things that made it are visible, so the graveyard of equally old failures goes uncounted.
- [[Chesterton's Fence]] shares Lindy's respect for longevity, reading survival as a hint of purpose.

## Who used it
Taleb uses Lindy as a filter for which books and ideas deserve attention. Physicist J. Richard Gott used a related "Copernican" argument in 1969 to estimate the Berlin Wall's remaining life, and in *Nature* (1993) to forecast closing dates for 44 Broadway shows. His reasoning is still debated.

## Trigger question
How long has this lasted, and is it the kind of thing that wears out?

## Often confused with
- [[Survivorship Bias]]: an error in sampling; Lindy is a claim about future lifespans.
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]]: Lindy is one specific prior, built from age alone.
