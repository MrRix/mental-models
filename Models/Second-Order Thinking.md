## What it is
Second-order thinking looks past the immediate effect of a decision to the effects of that effect. First-order thinking stops at what happens next; second-order thinking keeps asking "and then what?", including how people respond once the first effect lands.

## Origin
There is no single inventor, and the label is a modern umbrella term. The classic early statement is Frédéric Bastiat's 1850 essay "That Which Is Seen, and That Which Is Not Seen": a broken window employs the glazier, which is seen, while the shoes the shopkeeper would have bought are not. Ecologist Garrett Hardin's first law of human ecology says "We can never do merely one thing." Investor Howard Marks made "second-level thinking" the opening chapter of *The Most Important Thing* (2011): the question is not whether a company is good but whether the price already assumes it.

## Why it works
The first effect is the one everyone sees, so it is already argued over, planned for or priced in. Later effects are delayed, diffuse and sometimes opposite in sign, because people adapt to incentives and systems feed back on themselves. Walking the causal chain on purpose surfaces those reversals before they arrive.

## Best used when
- A new rule or price will change behaviour, and people will adapt to it or game it.
- The intervention lands in a complex system such as an ecosystem, a market or an organization.
- Short-term gain and long-term cost point in opposite directions.

## Limitations
- Forecast error compounds at every step; third-order effects are mostly guesswork.
- It can slide into slippery-slope storytelling or a sophisticated excuse for inaction.

## Avoid when
- The decision is cheap and reversible, so observing beats predicting.
- Speed matters more than precision.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Analysis Paralysis]]: the main tension, where endless chains of consequences freeze action.
- [[Opportunity Cost]]: Bastiat's unseen, in economic dress.
- [[Goodhart's Law]] and the [[Cobra Effect]]: classic second-order failures.

## Who used it
- Bastiat, against protectionism and make-work spending.
- Garrett Hardin, in ecology and population debates.
- Howard Marks at Oaktree Capital, in contrarian investing.

## Trigger question
And then what, and who reacts to it?

## Often confused with
- [[Inversion]]: reverses the question rather than extending it.
- [[Law of Unintended Consequences]]: the outcome second-order thinking tries to anticipate.
