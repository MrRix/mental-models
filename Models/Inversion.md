## What it is
Inversion attacks a problem from the far end. Instead of asking how to succeed, it asks what would guarantee failure; instead of proving a claim directly, it assumes the opposite and follows it until something breaks.

## Origin
The maxim "man muss immer umkehren" (one must always invert) is credited to the mathematician Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi (1804 to 1851), who built his theory of elliptic functions by inverting elliptic integrals. The attribution is shaky: historian Michael Molinsky (2016, reprinted in the MAA's *Convergence* in 2024) found the oldest source is a 1916 paper by Edward Van Vleck, which gives no source and only reports Jacobi as having said it. Charlie Munger made it famous: at a Harvard School graduation in Los Angeles on 13 June 1986, he credited Jacobi with "Invert, always invert" and listed prescriptions for a guaranteed life of misery.

## Why it works
Thinking forward from a goal anchors on the plan already in mind and hunts for reasons it will work. Flipping the question changes what the mind searches for. Failure modes are usually more concrete than paths to success, so they are easier to name, and a short list of things to avoid is often more reliable than a long list of things to do.

## Best used when
- The goal is fuzzy but failure is easy to picture.
- The downside matters more than the upside.
- The direct route to a solution or proof is stuck.

## Limitations
- Listing ways to fail does not produce a way to win. Avoiding stupidity can deliver average, not excellent.
- It can generate many risks with no sense of which are likely.

## Avoid when
- The upside is the whole point, as in exploratory or creative work.
- A clear forward procedure already exists.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[First Principles Thinking]]: pulls toward building a solution, where inversion pulls toward defence.
- [[Margin of Safety]]: the structural cousin, a buffer against the failures inversion names.
- [[Loss Aversion]]: what inversion feeds when defence hardens into fear of any downside.

## Who used it
- Carl Jacobi, in elliptic function theory.
- Charlie Munger, in his talks and at Berkshire Hathaway.
- Mathematicians generally, through proof by contradiction.

## Trigger question
What would guarantee failure here, and is any of it already happening?

## Often confused with
- [[Pre-Mortem]]: a structured exercise on one plan. Inversion is the broader habit.
- Pessimism: inversion is a search method, not a mood.
- [[Via Negativa]]: improvement by subtraction, often where inversion leads.

An airline safety board cuts accidents by cataloguing everything that makes a crash more likely, then banning each item. What way of thinking drives it?::[[Inversion]], working back from the disaster; its main counter-model is [[First Principles Thinking]], which builds the solution forward.
Asked for the secret of a happy life, a commencement speaker instead gives a detailed recipe for misery. Which model is he using, and what does it leave out?::[[Inversion]]; it says what to avoid but not what to build, which is the job of [[First Principles Thinking]].
Stuck on a direct proof, a mathematician assumes her theorem is false and derives an absurdity. What move did she make?::[[Inversion]], in the form of proof by contradiction; the counter-model is direct, forward reasoning in the style of [[First Principles Thinking]].
