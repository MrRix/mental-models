## What it is
Judging the quality of a decision by the quality of its outcome. Good results get credited to good choices and bad results to bad choices, as if luck and hidden information played no part.

## Origin
"Resulting" is poker slang. Former professional Annie Duke says poker players use the word, and her book *Thinking in Bets* (2018) brought it to a wide audience. Nobody has documented who coined it or when. Psychologists call it "outcome bias": Baron and Hershey (1988) showed people rated an identical medical or gambling decision as worse, and its maker as less competent, when told it turned out badly, though the decision maker had the same information either way.

## Why it works
Outcomes are vivid; the odds that stood behind a decision are invisible afterwards. In a single trial, skill and luck produce identical-looking results, and the result is all that is left to grade.

## Best used when
Reviewing decisions made under uncertainty with small samples: one hand, one trade, one hire.

## Limitations
Over many repetitions, results do carry information about skill, and dismissing every bad outcome as variance can shield a bad process. The effect itself has held up: a 2023 preregistered replication of Baron and Hershey (692 participants) confirmed it.

## Avoid when
Feedback is fast, plentiful and mostly skill-driven, as in chess, where the outcome is a fair grade.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Expected Value]]: grade the decision on what was knowable and the odds at the time.
- Tension: accountability runs on results, and a "good process" story can be invented after the fact.

## Who used it
- Duke opens her book with Pete Carroll's intercepted goal-line pass in Super Bowl XLIX (2015), mocked as one of the worst calls ever; she argues the critics were resulting.
- Robert Rubin, as Treasury Secretary, told Penn graduates in 1999 to judge decisions by how they were made, not only by results, citing a failed 1998 Russia program he still thought right.

## Trigger question
Would this call look different if the dice had landed the other way?

## Often confused with
- [[Hindsight Bias]]: misjudging how predictable the outcome was; resulting grades the decision by it.
- [[Survivorship Bias]]: drawing lessons only from the winners.
