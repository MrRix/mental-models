## What it is
People judge how frequent or likely something is by how easily examples come to mind. Ease of recall often tracks real frequency, but vivid, recent or heavily reported events come to mind more easily than their frequency warrants.

## Origin
Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, "Availability: A Heuristic for Judging Frequency and Probability" (*Cognitive Psychology*, 1973). Their best-known item asked whether letters such as K and R appear more often first or third in English words. Most people said first, because words are easier to search by their first letter, though these letters are more common in third position.

## Why it works
Retrieval is fast and cheap, and things met often are usually easier to recall. The shortcut breaks when recall has other causes: drama, media coverage, recency. Schwarz and colleagues (1991) showed the feeling of ease itself matters: people asked to list twelve examples of their own assertiveness, a hard task, rated themselves less assertive than people asked for six.

## Best used when
- Explaining gaps between public fear and actual risk.
- Spotting judgments driven by one recent or memorable case.

## Limitations
Sedlmeier, Hertwig and Gigerenzer (1998) found judgments of letter positions more accurate than availability predicts. Critics argue ease-of-retrieval findings are inflated by publication bias. The concept is loose enough to explain almost any result after the fact. And recall is often a decent guide: in the classic death-risk study, people broadly knew which causes were most and least common.

## Avoid when
- Memories come from a large, unbiased sample of experience with feedback.
- A frequency error has no plausible link to what is vivid or memorable.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]] replaces recalled examples with counted data.
- [[WYSIATI]] names the broader habit of judging only from what comes to mind.

## Who used it
Lichtenstein, Slovic and colleagues (1978) found people overestimated dramatic causes of death, judging homicide more frequent than diabetes or stomach cancer. Timur Kuran and Cass Sunstein (1999) described "availability cascades", where attention makes a risk more salient, which draws more attention, with Love Canal as a case study.

## Trigger question
Is this common, or just easy to picture?

## Often confused with
- [[Representativeness Heuristic]]: similarity to a type, not ease of recall.
- Recency bias: one source of availability, not the whole of it.
