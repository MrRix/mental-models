## What it is
Once the outcome is known, it seems to have been more predictable than it was. People overestimate what they would have predicted and misremember what they actually did predict.

## Origin
Baruch Fischhoff named it "creeping determinism" in a 1975 paper, *Hindsight ≠ foresight*. Participants read about an obscure 1814 war between the British and the Gurkhas; those told a given outcome had happened rated it more likely than those told nothing, even when that outcome was not the real one. The same year, Fischhoff and Ruth Beyth had people forecast events around Nixon's 1972 trips to China and the Soviet Union, then recall their forecasts; they remembered being more right than they were.

## Why it works
Outcome knowledge quietly rewrites what is known, and earlier judgments get reconstructed from the updated version rather than retrieved intact. A tidy causal story then makes the result feel inevitable, and the signals that pointed elsewhere fade.

## Best used when
Reviewing accidents, failures and lawsuits, where the judges know the ending and the original decision maker did not.

## Limitations
Robust but modest: meta-analyses (1991, 2004) report small to moderate effects, moderated by task familiarity. Hoffrage, Hertwig and Gigerenzer (2000) argue it is a by-product of efficient knowledge updating rather than a pure flaw. Warning people about it barely reduces it.

## Avoid when
Some outcomes really were foreseeable. Labelling every "should have known" as hindsight bias can excuse genuine negligence.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Pre-Mortem]]: borrows hindsight in advance by imagining the failure has already happened.
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]]: how often did such signs precede such outcomes, judged beforehand?

## Who used it
- Kamin and Rachlinski (1995): in a mock case, 76% of people judging in foresight thought a town's flood precautions unnecessary, while 57% of those told a flood happened called the same choice negligent. A debiasing instruction did not help.
- Radiology malpractice studies find expert witnesses overrate how visible a missed abnormality was.

## Trigger question
What was knowable before the ending?

## Often confused with
- [[Resulting]]: grading a decision by its outcome; hindsight bias misjudges how predictable that outcome was.
- [[Narrative Fallacy]]: building a neat causal story, often the vehicle for hindsight.
