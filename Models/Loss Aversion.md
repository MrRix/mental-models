## What it is
Losses feel bigger than gains of the same size. Losing $100 hurts more than winning $100 pleases, so people turn down fair bets and cling to what they have. Outcomes are judged as changes from a reference point, not as final wealth.

## Origin
Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, "Prospect Theory," *Econometrica*, 1979. Their value function is steeper for losses: "losses loom larger than gains." Their 1992 follow-up estimated a coefficient of 2.25, the source of the popular "losses hurt twice as much."

## Why it works
A reference point (the status quo, an expectation, a purchase price) splits outcomes into gains and losses, and the curve is steeper on the loss side, so equal gains and losses net out negative. Owning something moves the reference point, so giving it up registers as a loss. That asymmetry is used to explain the endowment effect, status quo bias, and refusals of favourable coin flips.

## Best used when
- Choices are framed as giving something up versus getting something.
- Stakes are large relative to the decider's wealth.

## Limitations
Its size, and sometimes its existence, is a live debate.
- David Gal and Derek Rucker (2018) argued it was stretched into a universal law; inertia may explain status quo and endowment findings.
- Several studies find little or none at small stakes.
- Defenders cite Mrkva and colleagues (2020; 17,720 participants), Ruggeri and colleagues' 19-country replication of prospect theory (2020), and a 2024 meta-analysis of 607 estimates averaging about 1.96.

## Avoid when
- Stakes are trivial or the "loss" is only a forgone gain.
- Inertia or plain risk aversion explains the choice equally well.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Expected Value]]: the textbook benchmark, where equal gains and losses weigh the same.
- Psychological inertia: the status quo wins because acting is costly.
- Loss attention (Yechiam and Hochman): losses grab attention without carrying more weight.

## Who used it
- Shlomo Benartzi and Richard Thaler (1995) used "myopic loss aversion" to explain the equity premium puzzle.
- Their Save More Tomorrow plan (2004) tied savings increases to pay rises so take-home pay never fell.

## Trigger question
Is this a real loss, or just a gain not taken?

## Often confused with
- Risk aversion, preferring a sure thing to an equal-value gamble, which needs no loss frame.
- [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]], narrower and partly explained by loss aversion.
