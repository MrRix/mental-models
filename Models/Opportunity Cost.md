## What it is
The real cost of a choice is the value of the best alternative given up to make it. Money, time and attention spent on one thing cannot be spent on another, so every "yes" carries a silent "no".

## Origin
Frédéric Bastiat's 1850 essay on "what is seen and what is not seen" already centred the unseen alternative. The English term appeared in January 1894, in David I. Green's "Pain-Cost and Opportunity-Cost" in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*. Friedrich von Wieser is often credited with coining it in 1914, but Green came twenty years earlier.

## Why it works
Options arrive one at a time, and the mind judges the one in front of it. The rival use of the money never shows up on an invoice, so it gets no vote. Naming the next-best alternative turns "is this good?" into "is this better than anything else these resources could do?", the only question a fixed budget can answer.

## Best used when
- Cash, hours or attention are genuinely scarce.
- An option looks attractive and nobody has named its rival.
- "Do nothing" is being treated as free.

## Limitations
- The best alternative is often unknown, so the cost is an estimate.
- Even experts stumble: in Ferraro and Taylor's 2005 survey, about 22% of economists at the American Economic Association meetings answered a simple concert-ticket question correctly, though critics call the question ambiguous.
- Frederick and colleagues (2009) found people neglect opportunity costs unless prompted. A 2023 meta-analysis confirmed the effect but found it much smaller than first reported.

## Avoid when
- Stakes are trivial and comparing costs more than the choice.
- Options do not compete for the same resource.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Satisficing]]: endless comparison breeds paralysis; good enough can beat best.
- [[Loss Aversion]]: visible losses loom larger than invisible forgone gains.
- [[Margin of Safety]]: slack that looks idle may be buying protection.

## Who used it
- Bastiat's broken-window parable: the glazier's gain is seen, the forgone shoes are not.
- Eisenhower's "Chance for Peace" speech (1953) priced one heavy bomber in brick schools for more than 30 cities.

## Trigger question
What is the best thing being given up to do this?

## Often confused with
- [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]]: sunk costs are past and irrelevant; opportunity costs are future and decisive.
- Accounting cost: the invoice amount, which ignores the forgone alternative.
