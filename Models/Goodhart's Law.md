## What it is
A number that tracks something people care about stops tracking it once people are rewarded or punished by it. Effort flows into moving the number instead of the thing it was meant to describe.

## Origin
Economist Charles Goodhart made the point in a 1975 Reserve Bank of Australia conference paper on British monetary policy. His version was technical: a statistical regularity tends to collapse once policymakers lean on it for control. The famous wording, "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure," is not his. It comes from anthropologist Marilyn Strathern's 1997 paper on university audits, summarizing accounting historian Keith Hoskin's gloss. Donald Campbell reached a similar idea about social indicators, and some scholars argue he got there first.

## Why it works
A measure is useful because, under normal conditions, it moves with the goal. Targeting it changes those conditions. The people measured now want to move the number by the cheapest route, which rarely runs through the goal: gaming, cutting corners on whatever is not counted, or redefining what counts. Each pays faster than real improvement, so the link that made the measure useful weakens.

## Best used when
- A metric is tied to pay, funding or rankings.
- A number improves faster than the outcome it stands for.
- The people measured also control how the data is produced.

## Limitations
It predicts that a targeted measure degrades, not by how much, so it cannot tell a slightly bent metric from a broken one. Many targets keep working tolerably, which the law alone does not explain.

## Avoid when
- The measure is the goal itself.
- The metric is hard to game or close to the real goal.
- The people measured cannot influence it.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[What Gets Measured Gets Managed]]: targets do focus effort. Goodhart describes a cost of targets, not a case against measuring.
- [[Incentive-Caused Bias]] is the individual-level engine behind the effect.

## Who used it
- British money-supply targets of the 1970s and 1980s, whose link to inflation unravelled once targeted.
- The UK's 2020 pledge of 100,000 COVID tests a day, met partly by counting home kits posted out.

## Trigger question
Is the thing improving, or only the number?

## Often confused with
- [[Campbell's Law]], the social-science version about corrupted indicators.
- The [[Lucas Critique]], which says historical relationships shift when policy changes.
- The [[Cobra Effect]], where an incentive worsens the original problem.

#flashcards
A hospital meets its emergency wait-time target by keeping patients in ambulances outside until a bed frees up. What happened to the number?::[[Goodhart's Law]]: once the wait time became a target it was gamed and stopped reflecting care. Counter-model: [[What Gets Measured Gets Managed]], since the target did push attention onto waits.
A call centre pays bonuses for short calls, and the same customers keep ringing back with the same problem. What went wrong?::[[Goodhart's Law]]: call length was a proxy for good service and got optimized at the expense of solved problems. Counter-model: [[What Gets Measured Gets Managed]], which argues for a better measure, not none.
A central bank adopts a money-supply figure as its target because it has long predicted inflation, and within a few years the link falls apart. What broke it?::[[Goodhart's Law]] in its original monetary form: leaning on the regularity for control destroyed it. Counter-model: [[What Gets Measured Gets Managed]], which holds only while the measure stays hard to game.
