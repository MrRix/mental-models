## What it is
"Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion." A task takes longer when more time is allowed, and organizations add staff whether or not the work grows.

## Origin
Naval historian C. Northcote Parkinson published it as a satirical essay in *The Economist* on 19 November 1955. The famous opening line is only the setup. Most of the essay concerns bureaucratic growth, which is what Parkinson called his law. His showcase: between 1914 and 1928, capital ships in commission fell from 62 to 20 while Admiralty officials rose about 78%. The personal-productivity reading popular today came later.

## Why it works
Parkinson offered two motives for the staff half: officials prefer adding subordinates to adding rivals, and those subordinates then make work for one another. For individual tasks, the deadline acts as a goal that sets the pace; spare time gets absorbed rather than banked. Bryan and Locke recast the law in 1967 as exactly this goal-setting effect.

## Best used when
- Deadlines or budgets are set generously by default.
- Headcount grows while output stays flat.

## Limitations
It began as satire, built on selective figures from a few departments. The time effect appears in some lab and field studies, but tight deadlines also raise errors, and some work needs slack for thinking. It does not say what the right amount of time is.

## Avoid when
- Work is fixed in size and externally paced.
- Slack is a buffer against genuine uncertainty.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Planning Fallacy]]: people underestimate time, so a generous allowance may simply be accurate.
- [[Margin of Safety]] defends deliberate slack as insurance, not waste.

## Who used it
- Parkinson, on the Admiralty and the Colonial Office, whose staff kept growing as the empire shrank.
- Latham and Locke (1975), reporting higher field productivity as time limits were cut.
- Klimek, Hanel and Thurner (2008), finding government performance tends to fall as cabinets grow, as Parkinson claimed.

## Trigger question
Is this taking as long as it needs, or as long as it was given?

## Often confused with
- [[Student Syndrome]], starting late and finishing at the deadline.
- [[Law of Triviality]], his separate claim that committees spend longest on the cheapest items.
- [[Brooks's Law]], about added people slowing late work.
