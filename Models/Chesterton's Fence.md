## What it is
Before removing a rule, process or structure that seems pointless, find out why it was put there. Once the reason is known, removal may be fine; until then, failing to see its use says something about the reformer, not the fence.

## Origin
G. K. Chesterton, English writer and Catholic apologist, set out the parable in *The Thing* (London, 1929), opening the essay "The Drift from Domesticity". A hasty reformer wants a fence across a road gone because he sees no use for it; a wiser one replies, "Go away and think," and allows removal once the reformer returns knowing its purpose. The snappier "Don't ever take a fence down until you know the reason why it was put up" is a paraphrase; Bartlett's traces it to a 1945 John F. Kennedy notebook crediting Chesterton.

## Why it works
Rules are usually built to solve a problem someone once had. When the fix works, the problem disappears from view and the fix starts to look like clutter: success erases its own evidence. Asking for the original reason recovers knowledge the system holds but nobody remembers.

## Best used when
- Inheriting an old codebase, policy or process.
- A rule looks absurd but has survived a long time.
- Finding out is cheap compared with breaking something.

## Limitations
- Some fences have no recoverable reason, and research can stall forever.
- Reasons go stale: knowing why a fence exists does not mean it should stay.
- It can become a rhetorical shield against any change.

## Avoid when
- The fence causes clear, present harm.
- Removal is cheap and reversible, so testing beats archaeology.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[First Principles Thinking]]: rebuilds from scratch rather than honouring inherited structure.
- [[Second-Order Thinking]]: asks what happens after the fence is gone.
- [[Lindy Effect]]: treats survival as weak evidence of usefulness.

## Who used it
- John F. Kennedy, who copied the paraphrase into a 1945 notebook.
- Wikipedia editors, whose essay "Chesterton's fence" asks for research before deletions or policy changes.

## Trigger question
Do I know why this exists, or only that I can't see why?

## Often confused with
- Status quo bias: preferring what exists; the fence asks only for understanding.
- [[Lindy Effect]]: a claim about longevity, not purpose.
