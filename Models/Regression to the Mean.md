## What it is
When a result is extreme partly through luck, the next one tends to land closer to average. Nothing pulls it back; the luck simply fails to repeat. The more chance in an outcome, the stronger the effect.

## Origin
Francis Galton found it in heredity. In an 1877 lecture he reported that large sweet pea seeds produced offspring seeds closer to average, calling it "reversion." He renamed it "regression" in 1885, and in "Regression towards Mediocrity in Hereditary Stature" (1886) showed the same with human height. Galton first took it for a law of heredity; it is a statistical consequence of any imperfect correlation.

## Why it works
Every result is a stable component (skill, true value) plus noise. Selecting on extremes selects for unusual noise. On remeasurement the noise is redrawn, so the extreme group drifts toward the middle. The overall spread does not shrink; new extremes appear elsewhere.

## Best used when
- Judging change after selecting the best or worst: top funds, failing schools, the sickest patients.
- Something was done right after an extreme result and seemed to work.

## Limitations
- It needs imperfect correlation; for stable, precisely measured traits the effect is small.
- It does not say how far toward the mean without a model of how much is noise.

## Avoid when
- The underlying process has genuinely changed: a new manager, a rule change, an injury.
- No one selected on extremes.

## Counter-models and tensions
- Cumulative advantage: some successes compound rather than revert, as in [[Reflexivity]] and the [[Lindy Effect]].
- [[Representativeness Heuristic]]: intuition expects the next result to resemble the last.

## Who used it
- Israeli Air Force flight instructors told Kahneman that praise made cadets worse and shouting made them better. Both followed extreme performances; regression did the rest.
- Harold Hotelling's 1933 review showed Horace Secrist's *The Triumph of Mediocrity in Business* had mistaken regression for economics.
- Clinical trials use control groups partly because patients enrolled at their worst improve anyway.

## Trigger question
Was this picked because it was extreme?

## Often confused with
- Gambler's fallacy, the belief that independent outcomes must balance out. Regression needs no balancing, only fresh luck.
- [[Resulting]]: judging a decision by its outcome.
