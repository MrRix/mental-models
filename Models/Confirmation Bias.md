## What it is
The tendency to seek, interpret and remember evidence in ways that favour what is already believed. It shapes which questions get asked, which answers count, and which ones quietly get forgotten.

## Origin
Francis Bacon noted in 1620 that the mind, once settled on an opinion, "draws all things else to support and agree with it." The experimental origin is Peter Wason's 1960 2-4-6 task: people guessing a hidden number rule mostly tested triples that fit their hunch, rarely ones that could break it. Wason is often credited with coining the term, but that paper does not use it; it was in print by 1977, in the title of a paper by Mynatt, Doherty and Tweney.

## Why it works
- Looking for hits is cheap and feels like progress; looking for misses feels like undermining oneself.
- Ambiguous evidence gets read through the existing belief, so it counts as support.
- Contrary facts get scrutinized harder and remembered worse, so the ledger tilts over time.

## Best used when
Explaining why capable people stay wrong: investigations locked onto an early suspect, rivals reading the same study as a win.

## Limitations
The label lumps different things together. Klayman and Ha (1987) showed much of Wason's result is a "positive test strategy," often a sound heuristic that fails mainly when the true rule is broader than the guess. A famous offshoot, backlash against counter-evidence, found no support in three large experiments by Guess and Coppock (2020).

## Avoid when
The prior is well grounded and the contrary evidence is weak. Calling every firm view confirmation bias is its own lazy move.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Falsifiability]]: hunt for the result that would sink the idea.
- [[Pre-Mortem]] and [[Inversion]] force the contrary case into view.
- [[Bayesian Updating]]: holding priors is not automatically bias.

## Who used it
Charles Darwin's *Autobiography* describes a "golden rule": he noted any fact opposed to his general results at once, because such facts slipped from memory faster than favourable ones.

## Trigger question
What would prove this wrong, and has anyone looked?

## Often confused with
- [[Motivated Reasoning]]: driven by wanting a conclusion; confirmation bias appears even with no stake.
- [[WYSIATI]]: reasoning only from what is in view, without hunting for support.
