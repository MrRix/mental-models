## What it is
Everyone has a zone where their knowledge is deep enough to judge well, and a much larger zone where it is not. The edge of that zone matters more than its size: an expert's advantage holds inside the boundary and vanishes outside it.

## Origin
It comes from Berkshire Hathaway. Charlie Munger used the phrase in a 1994 talk at USC's business school, saying "Every person is going to have a circle of competence." Warren Buffett's 1996 shareholder letter made it famous: the size of the circle matters less than knowing its boundaries. The motto "Know your circle of competence, and stick within it" appears in later retellings, not in the letter. Earlier uses have not been pinned down.

## Why it works
Judgment rests on pattern recognition built from feedback. Inside a familiar domain, errors get spotted and corrected quickly; outside it, confidence carries over while accuracy does not. The boundary separates evidence-backed calls from hunches that merely feel the same.

## Best used when
- Stakes are high and outcomes hard to reverse.
- An expert in one field weighs in on another.
- Counterparties know the domain better.

## Limitations
- People misjudge their own boundaries, and overconfidence is most tempting near the edge.
- Staying inside means missing things. Buffett has said he misses "a lot of games" and does not count Amazon as a terrible mistake.
- Circles can grow, and treating them as fixed freezes learning.

## Avoid when
- The domain is new to everyone, so nobody has a circle.
- Cheap experiments are the fastest way to build competence.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Optionality]]: small bets outside the circle buy information.
- [[Margin of Safety]]: the backup when the boundary is uncertain.
- [[Confirmation Bias]]: keeps the circle looking bigger than it is.

## Who used it
- Buffett and Munger, who for decades avoided most technology stocks they said they could not value.
- Buffett's 1996 letter: an investor need not be an expert on every company, or even many.

## Trigger question
Has my judgment actually been tested on this kind of problem?

## Often confused with
- [[Dunning-Kruger Effect]]: a claim about miscalibration, not a rule about boundaries.
- Specialization: choosing a focus, not knowing where judgment stops.
