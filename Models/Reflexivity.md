## What it is
In markets and societies, what participants believe changes the facts they are trying to understand. Beliefs move prices, prices move fundamentals, and the loop can feed on itself until it overshoots and reverses.

## Origin
George Soros set out the idea in *The Alchemy of Finance* (1987). He traces it to his student days at the London School of Economics, where Karl Popper's stress on imperfect knowledge clashed with economics' assumption of fully informed actors. Older relatives include Robert Merton's 1948 "self-fulfilling prophecy," where a rumour topples a sound bank, and Popper's "Oedipus effect."

## Why it works
Soros separates two functions of thinking: the cognitive, which tries to understand the world, and the manipulative, which tries to change it. When both run at once, belief has no fixed reality to anchor to, because acting on it shifts the target. In property, easy credit raises collateral values, which justifies more lending, which raises values further. The misconception that collateral is independent of credit holds until it does not; then the same loop runs in reverse.

## Best used when
- Prices feed back into fundamentals: share-funded acquisitions, credit and property, currency pegs.
- A trend and a popular misreading of it reinforce each other.

## Limitations
Soros calls his framework "still very rudimentary." It does not say when the turn comes, so it reads more easily in hindsight, and almost any reversal can be described as the loop breaking. By his own account it made little impression on academic economics.

## Avoid when
- Players are small relative to deep markets, so their beliefs barely move anything.
- Fundamentals sit beyond belief's reach, such as rainfall.

## Counter-models and tensions
- The [[Efficient Market Hypothesis]] treats prices as reflecting information, not reshaping it.
- [[Regression to the Mean]] predicts reversion; reflexivity predicts trends running further first.

## Who used it
- Soros, who tested it in a real-time diary of running his Quantum Fund (1985 to 1986), published in the book.
- His analyses of the 1982 sovereign debt crisis and the 2007 to 2008 credit "super-bubble."

## Trigger question
Is this belief describing the situation, or changing it?

## Often confused with
- [[Self-Fulfilling Prophecy]], a one-way case where a belief makes itself true.
- [[Herd Behavior]], copying others without feedback into fundamentals.
- [[Goodhart's Law]], a reflexive loop specific to metrics.

A company's soaring share price lets it buy rivals with stock, which lifts reported earnings and the share price again. What loop is running?::[[Reflexivity]]: the price is changing the fundamentals it supposedly reflects. Counter-model: the [[Efficient Market Hypothesis]], where price only mirrors value.
Banks point to rising house prices as proof their loans are safe, while their own lending is what pushes prices up. What is the hidden flaw?::[[Reflexivity]]: collateral values depend on the credit they secure, so the loop can reverse. [[Regression to the Mean]] also predicts a fall, but not the overshoot first.
Speculators bet against a currency peg; defending it drains reserves, which makes the peg look weaker and draws more bets. Why can the attack succeed against a sound economy?::[[Reflexivity]]: the bets change the fundamentals, here the reserves, that they are betting on. Counter-model: the [[Efficient Market Hypothesis]], which treats the price as a verdict on fundamentals that already existed.
