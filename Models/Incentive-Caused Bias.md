## What it is
Judgment bends toward whatever a person is rewarded for believing, usually without the person noticing. The bias is sincere: the surgeon or broker genuinely sees the evidence favouring the option that pays.

## Origin
Charlie Munger named it in "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment," a Harvard talk that the transcript dates to about June 1995. He listed it third of 24 causes of misjudgment and tied it to what economists call agency costs. The idea is far older: Upton Sinclair made the point about salaries and understanding in the 1930s. Munger's contribution was the label and the insistence that it is mostly unconscious, not fraud.

## Why it works
The reward acts on the search, not the verdict. Through motivated reasoning, people look harder for evidence favouring the paying answer and judge it more kindly. Each step feels like honest thinking, so the conclusion arrives with full conviction. In experiments by Moore, Tanlu and Bazerman, people assigned to advise a buyer or seller leaned toward their side even in private estimates.

## Best used when
- Advice comes from someone paid by the outcome they recommend.
- Fees reward volume, such as commissions or cost-plus billing.
- A career depends on a project continuing.

## Limitations
This is Munger's term, not a standard psychology construct; the evidence sits under motivated reasoning and conflict-of-interest research. Disclosure can backfire: Cain, Loewenstein and Moore (2005) found it made advisers more biased, though later work questioned how far that lab result generalizes.

## Avoid when
- Pay and the honest answer point the same way.
- A guessed motive is replacing a check of the evidence.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Hanlon's Razor]] offers stupidity as the alternative to malice. This bias adds a third explanation: sincere judgment, quietly bought.
- Incentives also fix behaviour: in Munger's FedEx story, paying night workers by the shift sped up sorting. Same lever, opposite effect.

## Who used it
- Munger's cases: a surgeon removing healthy gall bladders out of conviction, and Xerox salesmen pushing an inferior machine because commissions favoured it.
- Warren Buffett, quoting "a venerable caution" on Wall Street deal advice in his 2019 letter: "Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut."

## Trigger question
What would they believe if they were paid for the opposite answer?

## Often confused with
- [[Confirmation Bias]], which needs no reward.
- The [[Principal-Agent Problem]], the structure rather than the distortion.

A contractor paid a percentage of total cost earnestly recommends the most elaborate renovation on offer. Is the contractor lying?::Probably not: [[Incentive-Caused Bias]] bends honest judgment toward the fee. Counter-model: [[Hanlon's Razor]] would blame incompetence, but this explanation needs neither malice nor stupidity.
A financial adviser tells a client up front that she earns a commission on a fund, then recommends it more firmly than before. Why didn't telling the client help?::[[Incentive-Caused Bias]]: disclosure can make advice more biased, not less (Cain, Loewenstein and Moore). It is not [[Confirmation Bias]], since the lean follows the pay rather than a prior belief.
A team lead whose promotion rides on a project insists, month after month, that the failing project is nearly fixed. What explains the optimism?::[[Incentive-Caused Bias]]: the promotion shapes how the evidence looks. The rival explanation is the [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]], which would predict the same optimism with no promotion at stake.
