## What it is
People judge how likely something is by how much it resembles a typical case, or the process believed to produce it. Resemblance feels like probability, so prior odds, sample size and the rules of probability get neglected.

## Origin
Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, "Subjective Probability: A Judgment of Representativeness" (*Cognitive Psychology*, 1972). Judgments about samples followed salient features, such as a proportion, while largely ignoring sample size. Their 1971 paper "Belief in the Law of Small Numbers" was a precursor. Later work extended the idea to base-rate neglect (1973) and the 1983 "Linda problem", where most people rated "bank teller and feminist" more probable than "bank teller" alone.

## Why it works
Similarity is computed instantly; probability requires counting. When a description fits a stereotype, the fit quietly substitutes for the harder question of how common the category is. The result is a family of predictable errors: neglected base rates, insensitivity to sample size, conjunction errors, and expecting short random sequences to look random.

## Best used when
- Analysing judgments built on a vivid profile or description.
- Explaining overreaction to small samples.

## Limitations
Gerd Gigerenzer and Ralph Hertwig argued the Linda results partly reflect how people interpret the word "probability", and that conjunction errors shrink in frequency formats. Kahneman and Tversky replied that errors persist in some frequency versions; the debate is unresolved. The 1985 "hot hand" study, long cited as representativeness in action, was overturned by Miller and Sanjurjo (2018), whose correction revealed real streak shooting. The 1972 problems held up: a 2024 preregistered replication reproduced eight of nine.

## Avoid when
- The resemblance is itself strong evidence, as with a pattern seen thousands of times with feedback.
- Base rates are unknown or categories poorly defined.

## Counter-models and tensions
- [[Base Rates and the Outside View]] is the direct correction.
- [[Regression to the Mean]] explains why extreme samples mislead.

## Who used it
Tversky and Kahneman's 1971 questionnaire showed professional research psychologists expected small samples to mirror their populations, breeding overconfident conclusions. Gilovich, Vallone and Tversky (1985) applied the idea to basketball streaks, a case later reversed.

## Trigger question
It fits the type, but how common is the type?

## Often confused with
- [[Availability Heuristic]]: ease of recall, not similarity.
- Stereotyping: representativeness is the probability judgment a stereotype feeds.
